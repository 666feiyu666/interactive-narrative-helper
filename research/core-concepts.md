# Core Concepts

These working definitions make the research question observable. They remain
open to revision through documented research decisions.

## Macro design exploration concepts

### Creator-described educational purpose

The learning, reflection, awareness, practice, or behavior-related intention a
creator explicitly attributes to a work on a public project page. It records
the creator's positioning rather than an independently measured outcome.

### Intended audience

The learner, player, facilitator, or other user group that the creator
explicitly names or clearly addresses. Target learners should remain distinct
from page addressees, purchasers, and facilitators where the source supports
that distinction.

### Application setting

The creator-described context in which the work is intended to be used, such as
classroom, independent learning, museum, professional training, home, or public
engagement. A story setting or development context is not automatically an
application setting.

### Interactive narrative form

The creator-described way a player participates in or influences the narrative,
including choice, dialogue, parser input, role play, exploration, simulation,
state change, consequence feedback, replay, or ending variation.

### Educational–interaction relationship

A creator-described connection between an interaction and an intended
educational or reflective experience. Examples include using consequence
feedback to compare decisions or role play to support perspective-taking.

### Design precedent

A traceable case showing how a creator combined educational purpose, audience,
setting, and interactive form. A precedent is material for comparison and
adaptation, not a claim that the combination is universally effective.

### Educational Interactive Narrative Design Brief

A human-reviewed bridge between macro design exploration and a concrete story
task. It records selected educational and interaction directions together with
author constraints. Its program role is confirmed, but its machine-readable
contract is not yet fixed.

## Story-level narrative technique concepts

### Story-level narrative technique

A bounded way of transforming how a concrete story develops or is experienced
without delegating full-story authorship to the Agent. A technique may organize
changes in events and consequences, perspective and information, time and
sequence, or role and agency. These examples identify a conceptual space; they
are not current implemented modules.

### Technique-specific transformation

An inspectable proposal that applies one declared narrative technique to a
concrete story under author-defined invariants, permitted changes, and
exploration bounds. The proposal should expose what changes, what remains
stable, why the transformation is relevant, and what it may imply for
interaction, experience, interpretation, and educational purpose.

### Experiential possibility

A materially different way a player may encounter or participate in a story,
even when the underlying events do not change. Examples may include inhabiting
another character's position, revisiting an event, or taking a different role
in how the story is disclosed.

### Epistemic possibility

A change in what the player can know, when they can know it, whose account they
can access, or how partial and conflicting information can be compared. It is
not by itself evidence that a particular learning outcome occurs.

### Interpretive possibility

A supported alternative way of understanding a story that arises from the
designed experience rather than from an ungrounded moral appended to it. The
Partner presents interpretations for author review rather than declaring one
correct meaning.

### Technique framing

The author-controlled definition of which narrative operation is being
explored, what design intention it serves, which source constraints apply, and
what kinds of change are allowed. Technique framing belongs to the task, not to
an unconstrained model inference.

## Track B1 — Counterfactual technique concepts

### Canonical action

A consequential action performed by a character in the source story that helps
produce the familiar plot or ending. The action is described together with the
character's immediate goal and relevant story conditions.

### Counterfactual question

A bounded prompt that removes, replaces, or modifies a canonical action while
declaring which source-story facts remain fixed. Example: “What if the fox does
not use deception to obtain the cheese?”

### Alternative action

A strategy a character could take under the counterfactual conditions. An
alternative is substantive when its action logic or relationship to other
characters differs from the canonical strategy; a synonym or stylistic rewrite
does not count as a new action.

### Source compatibility

The degree to which a proposal respects declared invariants, character goals,
world rules, and recognizable story identity. Compatibility does not require
reproducing the canonical plot.

### Consequence projection

An inspectable causal hypothesis connecting an alternative action to immediate
reactions, intermediate developments, transformed conflict, further choices,
and possible endings. A fluent ending without a traceable causal path is not a
sufficient projection.

### Interactive potential

The degree to which a proposal creates meaningful opportunities for subsequent
player decisions whose consequences differ in state, relationship, knowledge,
or outcome. Producing a new linear plot is not by itself interactive design.

### Thematic shift

A possible reinterpretation of the story's concerns that arises from changed
actions and consequences. It must be presented as a supported reading, not as a
single objectively correct theme.

### Educational implication

A possible learning or reflective opportunity created by the experience of
choosing and observing consequences. It must be grounded in the branch design
and must not be inferred solely from a moralizing statement appended to the
story.

### Author utility

The extent to which a proposal helps an author discover, understand, select,
revise, combine, or develop narrative directions with acceptable effort.

### Author control

The author's continued authority over constraints, interpretation, selection,
revision, and final design. More Agent output is not automatically more author
control.
